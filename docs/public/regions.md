# The 14 regions — a corpus-first map of the world's mythologies

Mythoscope organises every tradition it holds under one top-level classification:
**`region`**, a set of **fourteen** values. The model has two levels — **`region` →
`tradition`** — where a region groups many traditions and a tradition is a single mythology
such as Greek, Norse, or Yoruba. `region` is the one primary axis of the catalogue; there is
no competing facet layer of language family or subsistence mode above it.

This page sets out why the regions are drawn the way they are, lists all fourteen with their
traditions, and explains the colour system that renders them.

## Why the regions are drawn from the corpus, not the index

The taxonomy is built on an **assembled full-text corpus**, not on a motif index. That single
commitment shapes everything downstream:

- **"From the data" means differences between the texts themselves** — their content,
  language, and genre — not counts of motif attestations.
- **The areal scaffolding is provisional.** Berezkin's areal groupings are a useful starting
  scaffold, not the authority; where the corpus asks for a different partition, traditions are
  re-annotated to serve it.
- **Obtainability constrains what fills each region first.** What full text can actually be
  acquired sets the pace: the literate Old World is build-ready, while the oral world waits on
  OCR and curation.

Every candidate region was judged against four criteria that routinely conflict:

1. **Geography** — does the region map to a compact, unambiguous area?
2. **Cultural commonality** — are its traditions genuinely kin (language, religion, descent,
   society type), not merely neighbours?
3. **Expressiveness and clarity** — recognizable names, a sensible count, no artificial merges.
4. **Volume of traditions and material** — balance measured against the *corpus*, by number of
   traditions and actual full-text volume; no empty regions, no overloaded heaps.

The scheme is the **cultural-areal** answer. It deliberately chose that over a
motif-volume-balanced scheme (abandoned the moment the project went corpus-first) and over a
purely text-weighted scheme (the future shape if the catalogue is ever re-weighted by text,
splitting the heavyweight literate regions).

### The central tension: distinctiveness versus documentation

Beneath the four criteria sits one dominant tension that sets the granularity of the whole
scheme. Two forces pull region size in opposite directions:

- **Cultural distinctiveness** would cut finely where diversity is highest — and diversity is
  highest in the **oral** world. Sub-Saharan Africa alone spans three-plus unrelated language
  phyla.
- **Volume of documentation** would cut finely where the **text** is deepest — and text
  concentrates in the **literate** Old World (South Asia, the Near East, East Asia, Europe).

These point against each other: the most culturally distinct regions are the least documented,
and the best-documented are internally the most uniform. Where they conflict, **documentation
wins the granularity call** — the literate spheres are split fine (South / Southeast / East /
Inner Asia), while the more diverse oral regions stay pooled (one Sub-Saharan Africa, one
Lowland South America) with their internal diversity demoted to sub-rubrics. This asymmetry is
deliberate and load-bearing. By pure distinctiveness the first region to split would be
**Sub-Saharan Africa, not any part of Asia or Europe**. Keeping the axis explicit is what stops
the scheme from being misread as a claim that Asia is "more diverse" than Africa; Asia is only
better recorded.

### Hard lines through soft gradients

Mythological and cultural variation is **clinal** — a bundle of overlapping gradients
(language, subsistence, religion, descent, diffusion) that shade continuously into one another,
not a mosaic of sharp cells. Any 14-region partition therefore draws hard lines across soft
gradients. Regions are well-founded where several gradients bundle and steepen together into a
cultural watershed: the Sahara, the Indosphere/Sinosphere seam, the Austronesian sea-frontier,
the Bering crossing. They are more arbitrary where a single gradient is shallow — the Caucasus
shatter zone, the Andean–Amazon flank, the temperate-to-Arctic transition. The **boundary
cases** are not failures but exactly the places where the cline is caught mid-slope; each is
assigned by whichever gradient we privilege and openly flagged, so the underlying continuity is
never mistaken for a wall. This is also why an internal spread (Europe: Homer to the Kalevala
to Basque) can exceed a between-region gap (South Asia to mainland Southeast Asia, both
Indosphere).

### A synchronic cut through layered material

The region scheme is a **horizontal slice through a vertically stratified reality**. Every
region is a stack of historical layers — a *substrate → expansion(s) → literate or colonial
overlay* sequence, recorded with datings in each region's **Strata** below (Khoisan forager
substrate → Bantu expansion → Sahelian Islam → Christian overlay; Proto-Indo-European steppe →
Classical antiquity → Christianization → Eddic fixation). Three consequences follow:

- **The same ground belongs to different regions at different depths.** The map colours the
  *deepest attested indigenous stratum*, not the modern political surface — which is why
  Madagascar reads Austronesian and the Caribbean reads with lowland South America.
- **A region's coherence lives at one particular stratum.** Austronesia coheres at the
  Neolithic maritime-expansion layer, the Near East at the Bronze-Age-and-earlier layer,
  Europe at the Proto-Indo-European layer. Compared at the wrong depth, a grouping can look
  wrong — modern Vietnam is Sinicized on the surface but Austroasiatic in the substrate.
- **Space and time are two axes of one catalogue.** `region` partitions space; the dated
  strata partition time.

### Ordering: the out-of-Africa arc

The fourteen are ordered as a human-dispersal arc: cradle → exit corridor → the whole
contiguous Old World → one deliberate seam → the Americas, ending at the terminal tip of
settlement. Every Old-World transition is geographically contiguous. A linear list must break
once — the Eurasian trunk has two dead-end arms, the southern maritime arm to Australia and the
northern arm to Bering — and that single seam is placed at the Old-World/New-World boundary,
between **Papua & Aboriginal Australia** and **Circumpolar North**, the most meaningful location
for it.

## The fourteen regions

Each region's full entry in the catalogue carries a description, a subdivision into sub-rubrics,
its dated strata, and its list of traditions. The table below gives the description in brief with
example traditions; the notes beneath it record the decisions that shaped the harder calls.

| # | Region | In brief | Example traditions |
|---|--------|----------|--------------------|
| 1 | **Sub-Saharan Africa** | Africa south of the Sahara — the world's densest concentration of distinct oral traditions, spanning several unrelated language phyla; kept as one region because both candidate halves are text-thin and encyclopedias treat it as one. | Yoruba, Igbo, Akan/Ashanti, Fon, Dogon, Zulu, Shona, Maasai, Dinka, San, Mbuti |
| 2 | **Near East & North Africa** | The Ancient Near East plus North Africa and the Abrahamic homelands — the earliest literate mythologies (cuneiform, hieroglyphic) and the source of the three Abrahamic religions; includes Anatolia and the Maghreb. | Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, Egyptian, Hittite, Ugaritic, Jewish, Christian, Islamic, Berber |
| 3 | **Europe** | The mythologies of Europe — predominantly Indo-European with Uralic Finno-Ugric, sharing deep Proto-Indo-European roots; one region, with the Classical vs Northern/Eastern division kept as a sub-rubric. | Greek, Roman, Norse, Celtic, Slavic, Baltic, Finnish, Sami, Basque |
| 4 | **Caucasus & Iran** | The Iranian/Persianate world plus the Caucasus — bound by Iranian threads (Zoroastrian, Scythian-Alan, Persianate); the Caucasus attaches here rather than standing alone as a thin shatter zone. | Persian/Zoroastrian, Scythian, Ossetian (Nart), Armenian, Georgian, Circassian, Chechen |
| 5 | **Inner Asia** | The Turco-Mongol nomadic steppe — pastoralist, shamanic, Tengrist, with its own great oral epics; named "Inner Asia" because the Persianate oasis sits in region 4. | Turkic/Tengrist, Kyrgyz (Manas), Kazakh, Yakut, Mongol (Geser), Buryat, Kalmyk (Jangar), Tibetan (Bon) |
| 6 | **South Asia** | The Indian subcontinent — the single largest textual tradition on Earth (Vedic corpus, Sanskrit epics, Puranas, Buddhist and Jain canons); the source of the Indosphere, distinct from its Indianized periphery. | Vedic, Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Dravidian, Munda/Santal, Sinhalese, Sikh |
| 7 | **Mainland Southeast Asia** | The Indianized mainland — Indochina and the hill peoples whose classical mythology is Hindu-Buddhist; on the Indic side of the Indosphere/Sinosphere seam. Vietnam sits here. | Burmese, Thai/Tai, Lao, Khmer, Vietnamese, Cham, Hmong-Mien, Karen |
| 8 | **East Asia** | The Sinosphere — China, Korea, Japan — sharing the Chinese classical, Taoist, Confucian and Mahayana-Buddhist textual world; the clean CJK core. | Chinese (Han, Taoist, Confucian, folk), Korean, Japanese (Shinto), Ryukyuan, Ainu |
| 9 | **Austronesia** | The Austronesian cultural area — one language family and one Neolithic maritime expansion from the Taiwan homeland through island Southeast Asia to the Pacific and Madagascar. | Taiwan Formosan, Javanese, Balinese, Batak, Filipino, Malay, Polynesian, Micronesian, Malagasy |
| 10 | **Papua & Aboriginal Australia** | The non-Austronesian deep-Pacific Sahul population — Papuan/highland New Guinea peoples and Aboriginal Australians (the Dreaming); distinguished from Austronesia by descent and antiquity. | Aboriginal Australian (Arrernte, Yolngu, Warlpiri, Pitjantjatjara), Papuan highland groups (Enga, Huli) |
| 11 | **Circumpolar North** | The continuous circumpolar Arctic/boreal belt across the Bering Strait — hunter-shaman cultures that geography joins but the continental boundary would tear; the bridge into the New World. | Chukchi, Koryak, Yukaghir, Evenki, Nenets, Ket, Inuit/Eskimo, Aleut, Na-Dene/Athabaskan, Northern Cree |
| 12 | **Native North America** | Indigenous North America outside the Arctic — Eastern Woodlands, Plains, Plateau, California, the Southwest, and the Northwest Coast. | Iroquois, Algonquian, Lakota/Sioux, Pawnee, Blackfoot, Navajo, Hopi, Zuni, Tlingit, Haida |
| 13 | **Mesoamerica & the Andes** | The high civilizations of the Americas — the two literate, state-level New-World myth complexes, with codices and colonial chronicles (Popol Vuh, Florentine Codex, Huarochirí Manuscript). | Aztec/Nahua, Maya, Mixtec, Zapotec, Olmec, Inca/Quechua, Aymara, Moche, Chibcha |
| 14 | **Lowland South America** | The tropical and temperate lowlands — Amazonia, the Guianas, the Gran Chaco, and the Southern Cone; the densest concentration of small-scale Amerindian oral traditions and the terminal end of the human expansion. | Tupí/Guaraní, Carib, Arawak, Ge, Yanomami, Tucano, Jivaro, Mapuche, Selk'nam |

*Example traditions are illustrative; the catalogue records the full membership of each region.*

### The decisions behind the harder calls

- **Europe is one region, not two.** A two-Europe split was an artefact of motif-volume
  balancing. By full text, Europe is smaller than the single Near East region and internally
  more uniform than one-region Sub-Saharan Africa; splitting it while Africa and Native North
  America stay whole would be Eurocentric over-resolution. The Classical/Northern division
  survives as a sub-rubric.
- **The Near East absorbs North Africa and Anatolia.** North Africa is Afroasiatic and
  Arab-Islamic, tied to the Near East; Anatolia is the Bronze-Age Ancient Near East. "Near
  East" is kept over "Middle East" because the material is predominantly ancient.
- **The Caucasus pairs with Iran.** Too thin and internally diverse to stand alone, the
  Caucasus attaches to the historically Iranian/Persianate world: the Nart epic has an
  Iranian-Alan core, and the Christian textual peoples (Armenian, Georgian) sit here too.
- **Asia is split by cultural sphere.** Mainland Southeast Asia is Indianized, so it belongs on
  the Indic side rather than with Sinic East Asia. South Asia is then its own region on the
  strength of both volume and civilizational distinctness, separate from the smaller mainland
  Southeast Asia.
- **Austronesia and Sahul are each their own region.** Austronesia honours a cohesive
  descent-based cultural area — one family, one maritime expansion from Taiwan. Splitting off
  the Pleistocene Sahul population (Papua plus Aboriginal Australia) both respects a distinct
  culture area and cures the Aboriginal-Australia singleton.
- **The Circumpolar North crosses Bering.** The Arctic/boreal belt is genuinely continuous;
  dividing it by continent would tear a real culture area, so it spans both sides of the strait.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa stays one region for now** — to be re-split later if finer African data
  arrives.

A short set of **boundary cases** is flagged rather than hard-resolved — Tibet (placed in Inner
Asia), the Ethiopian Horn (Sub-Saharan Africa), Ainu (East Asia), Sami (Europe), Cham (mainland
Southeast Asia), the Northwest Coast (Native North America), Mapuche (Lowland South America),
and Vietnam (mainland Southeast Asia). Each marks a place where the cline is caught mid-slope.

## The colour system

Each region carries a single canonical colour, chosen to be **intuitive** — the hue evokes the
place rather than tracing a spectral arc. Saffron for South Asia, cinnabar red for East Asia,
red-centre ochre for Aboriginal Australia, ocean teal for Austronesia, Tengri eternal-blue for
the Inner Asian steppe. This trades a strict global hue sequence for immediate legibility of
*what* a region is; the map's borders and labels carry the neighbour-to-neighbour distinction.

|---|--------|--------|--------|
| 1 | Sub-Saharan Africa | `#BF6B3A` | terracotta / savanna earth |
| 2 | Near East & North Africa | `#E0B45E` | desert sand & gold |
| 3 | Europe | `#4F7A4E` | temperate forest green |
| 4 | Caucasus & Iran | `#9E4A6E` | Persian wine / rose |
| 5 | Inner Asia | `#2E7CB8` | Tengri eternal-blue steppe sky |
| 6 | South Asia | `#E8952F` | saffron |
| 7 | Mainland Southeast Asia | `#3E9E64` | monsoon jungle green |
| 8 | East Asia | `#C0392B` | cinnabar / vermillion red |
| 9 | Austronesia | `#1596A8` | Pacific ocean teal |
| 10 | Papua & Aboriginal Australia | `#A9773F` | red-centre ochre |

### One base colour per region; per-tradition shades are derived

Only the fourteen region colours are canonical. A tradition does not store a colour of its own —
its shade is **derived at render time** from the region base, as a pure function of the region's
base colour, the tradition's position within the region, and how many traditions the region holds.
The legend therefore stays a clean fourteen-colour legend, and a tradition is always the same
colour wherever it appears.

The derivation works in **OKLCH**, a perceptually uniform colour space. It fixes the region's
**hue** and varies mainly **lightness** (adding a second chroma axis only when a region holds
enough traditions to need it), so every shade still reads unmistakably as its region while
individual traditions stay separable. Working in OKLCH rather than HSL matters: HSL lightness is
not perceptual — equal steps look unequal, and a region's hue-identity collapses toward the black
and white ends. The bands are re-derived per theme, lifted for dark backgrounds and lowered for
light ones, never a mechanical flip of one value into the other.

The payoff is that the palette is **colour-blind-robust by construction**. Regions are already
separated by hue in a validated fourteen-colour palette; within a region, ordering the shades
along a lightness axis means they survive all forms of dichromacy, because lightness is preserved
under every colour-vision deficiency. The separability is a property of how the shades are built,
not something bolted on afterwards.
