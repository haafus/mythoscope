# How the great encyclopedias carve the world

Every general reference work on mythology has to answer a question before it prints a single
entry: *how do you divide the world's myths into sections?* The answer is never neutral. Where
one work gives Greece, Rome, Egypt, and Mesopotamia each a chapter, it gives "the Two Americas"
or "Black Africa" a single one — collapsing thousands of distinct traditions into a heading. The
choice of divisions encodes an argument about which mythologies deserve resolution.

This page surveys the major encyclopedias, dictionaries, handbooks, and digital databases that
attempt worldwide or comparatively broad coverage of mythology and comparative religion, across
English, Russian, French, and German scholarship. For each it records the organizing principle —
how it partitions world mythology — and its theoretical stance. A cross-cutting synthesis then
compares how these works carve the world and why some traditions get dedicated sections while
others are pooled. The recurring finding is a **documentary bias**: sections track the density
of *written sources and prior scholarship*, not the number of traditions or the richness of the
mythology.

## Part I — Per-work profiles

### 1. New Larousse Encyclopedia of Mythology (Guirand / Graves)

- **Editor / contributors:** Félix Guirand (editor); introduction by Robert Graves; translated
  by Richard Aldington and Delano Ames. French original *Larousse mythologie générale*.
- **Editions:** First English edition 1959; "New" edition 1968; further printings incl. 1987
  (Prometheus / Hamlyn / Crescent).
- **Format:** Single large illustrated volume (~500 pp.), heavily illustrated — long a popular
  gateway reference.
- **Organizing principle:** **Thematic essays organized by region/culture** (not an A–Z
  dictionary). Chapters are narrative surveys, each covering one cultural tradition with
  subsections by pantheon/theme.
- **Top-level sections (~17–18):** Introduction; Prehistoric; Egyptian; Assyro-Babylonian;
  Phoenician; Greek; Roman; Celtic; Teutonic; Slavonic; Finno-Ugric; Ancient Persia; Indian;
  Chinese; Japanese; Mythology of the Two Americas; Oceanic; Mythology of Black Africa.
- **Stance:** Popular-scholarly rather than peer-reviewed; French-tradition, descriptive/
  narrative, strong on the classical Mediterranean and Europe. Its bias is the classic one:
  fine-grained for literate Old World traditions, coarse pooling of "the Two Americas," "Oceanic,"
  and "Black Africa" into single chapters.

### 2. The Mythology of All Races (Gray / MacCulloch, 13 vols)

- **Editors:** Louis Herbert Gray; John Arnott MacCulloch (co-editor, later volumes); George Foot
  Moore (consulting editor).
- **Publisher / dates:** Marshall Jones Company, Boston, 1916–1932.
- **Structure:** **One or more regions per volume**, each a monograph by a specialist:
  1. Greek and Roman — W. S. Fox (1916)
  2. Eddic — J. A. MacCulloch (1930)
  3. Celtic and Slavic — MacCulloch & Jan Máchal (1918)
  4. Finno-Ugric, Siberian — Uno Holmberg (1927)
  5. Semitic — S. H. Langdon (1931)
  6. Indian and Iranian — A. B. Keith & Albert Carnoy (1917)
  7. Armenian and African — M. H. Ananikian & Alice Werner (1925)
  8. Chinese and Japanese — J. C. Ferguson & Masaharu Anesaki (1928)
  9. Oceanic — R. B. Dixon (1916)
  10. North American — H. B. Alexander (1916)
  11. Latin American — H. B. Alexander (1920)
  12. Egyptian and Indo-Chinese — W. M. Müller & J. G. Scott (1918)
  13. Complete Index (1932)
- **Stance:** Early-20th-century scholarly monograph series, each volume by a recognized
  specialist (Keith on India, Langdon on Semitic, Anesaki on Japan). Historical-philological, now
  dated (terminology, race-era framing, "Indo-Chinese") but still cited for depth. The same coarse
  pooling: two whole continents get one author each; "Oceanic" and "African" each a single section.

### 3. Encyclopedia of Religion (Eliade; 2nd ed. Lindsay Jones) — Macmillan

- **Editor:** 1st ed. Mircea Eliade (editor-in-chief); 2nd ed. Lindsay Jones.
- **Editions:** 1st ed. Macmillan, 1987 — **16 volumes**, ~5 million words. 2nd ed. Macmillan
  Reference USA / Thomson Gale, 2005 — **15 volumes**. Retains most of the ~2,750 original entries
  plus ~600 new — roughly **3,300 entries**, each with bibliography.
- **Organizing principle:** **Alphabetical A–Z** of religions, deities, concepts, thinkers,
  rituals, and methods — comparative religion rather than mythology per se, but the single most
  authoritative cross-cultural reference for myth-in-religion.
- **Stance:** The flagship academic reference in religious studies; peer-reviewed. Eliade's vision
  was **phenomenological and non-reductionist** (the "sacred" as an irreducible category).
  Criticisms: methodologically uncritical universalism, sweeping generalizations, an alleged
  Western/Christian and "antihistorical" bias. The 2nd edition deliberately added historicist and
  social-scientific correctives.

### 4. Man, Myth & Magic (Richard Cavendish)

- **Editor:** Richard Cavendish (1930–2016); art direction Brian Innes.
- **Editions:** BPC Publishing, launched 1970 as a British weekly **partwork — 112 issues, ~1,000
  articles, ~5,000 illustrations**; bound as 24 volumes (1970); Marshall Cavendish 21-vol. revised
  (1995); Cavendish Square 5-vol. set (2014).
- **Organizing principle:** **Alphabetical A–Z** (later reissues regroup thematically).
- **Coverage / stance:** Magic, mythology, religion, folklore, occult, the supernatural. Popular
  but credentialed — over 200 academic contributors including **E. R. Dodds, Mircea Eliade, Glyn
  Daniel, C. A. Burland**. Strongest as a mid-century survey of Western esotericism, folklore, and
  the supernatural rather than a rigorous myth reference.

### 5. Oxford & Routledge dictionaries of world / classical mythology

- **A Dictionary of World Mythology (Arthur Cotterell, OUP):** 1979/1986, Oxford Paperback
  Reference 1997. Organizing principle unusual — **region-grouped rather than strictly A–Z**:
  seven sections — **West Asia, South & Central Asia, East Asia, Europe, America, Africa,
  Oceania** — with entries inside each. Single-author, concise, general-audience.
- **The Oxford Dictionary of Classical Myth and Religion (Price & Kearns, 2003):** drawn from the
  *Oxford Classical Dictionary*; A–Z; Greek and Roman myth, religion, cult places, texts,
  divination, astrology, magic. Peer-reviewed, authoritative for the classical world only.
- **Oxford Classical Dictionary (OCD)** and its online form (oxfordre.com/classics): the standard
  academic reference for Greco-Roman antiquity, with a long "mythology" survey article.
- **Routledge dictionaries:** *The Routledge Dictionary of Gods and Goddesses, Devils and Demons*
  (Manfred Lurker) — **A–Z, over 1,800 deities/demons worldwide**; plus region-specific volumes,
  e.g. *A Dictionary of Ancient Near Eastern Mythology* (Gwendolyn Leick), *Routledge Dictionary of
  Egyptian Gods and Goddesses* (George Hart).

### 6. Funk & Wagnalls Standard Dictionary of Folklore, Mythology and Legend (Maria Leach)

- **Editors:** Maria Leach (editor); Jerome Fried (associate editor).
- **Editions:** Funk & Wagnalls; originally 2 vols. 1949–1950 (~1,196 pp.); one-volume reissue 1972
  (~1,236 pp., marketed "3rd ed."); later HarperCollins reprint. **~4,000 entries** in the standard
  set (some expanded figures cite up to ~8,000 articles).
- **Organizing principle:** **Alphabetical A–Z**, blending folklore, mythology, and legend — gods,
  heroes, fairies, demons, ballads, folk customs — across world cultures.
- **Stance:** Landmark **folkloristic** reference (mid-20th c.); signed articles by folklorists and
  anthropologists. Comparative folklore emphasis rather than a pantheon-by-pantheon encyclopedia;
  still cited, though dated.

### 7. Encyclopædia Britannica — mythology coverage

- **Form:** Not a dedicated myth encyclopedia but the long survey article **"Myth"** (sections on
  "Approaches to the study of myth and mythology," "Functions of myth and mythology"), plus
  per-tradition articles (Greek religion, Norse mythology, etc.).
- **Stance:** Signed scholarly overviews with extensive bibliographies; the "Myth" article is a
  respected synthesis of theoretical approaches (including Eliade's). Thematic/analytical rather
  than a catalog of deities.

### 8. Мифы народов мира (Myths of the Peoples of the World) — S. A. Tokarev, ed.

- **Chief editor:** S. A. Tokarev; deputy editor **E. M. Meletinsky**; contributors include
  **Vyach. Vs. Ivanov, V. N. Toporov, I. M. Diakonov, A. F. Losev, S. S. Averintsev**.
- **Editions:** Soviet Encyclopedia, 1st ed. 1980–1982 in **2 volumes**; 2nd ed. 1987–1988;
  reprints to 2003; electronic ed. 2008. **>12,000 entries.** Awarded the **USSR State Prize
  (1990).**
- **Organizing principle:** **Alphabetical A–Z**, but distinctive in that entries cover both
  individual figures/motifs *and* large synthetic articles on the mythology of each people/region
  and on theoretical topics (myth, ritual, cosmogony).
- **Stance:** One of the most ambitious and rigorous single attempts at a comprehensive, systematic
  account of world mythmaking. Strong **structuralist and comparative** (Moscow–Tartu semiotic
  school) methodology. Soviet-era ideological context is present, but the scholarship is highly
  respected internationally — and notably broad and even in its coverage of non-literate and
  Eurasian traditions that Western works pool coarsely.

### 9. Dictionnaire des mythologies (Yves Bonnefoy) — French; and English *Mythologies*

- **Full title:** *Dictionnaire des mythologies et des religions des sociétés traditionnelles et
  du monde antique* (dir. Yves Bonnefoy), Flammarion, 2 vols., 1981.
- **English edition:** *Mythologies*, "a restructured translation," translated under the direction
  of **Wendy Doniger**, University of Chicago Press, 1991, 2 vols. **395 articles, ~100
  contributors.**
- **Organizing principle — a key contrast:** the French original is **alphabetical**; the English
  *Mythologies* is **restructured geographically/culturally**:
  - **Vol. 1:** Africa; The Ancient Near East; Celts, Norse, Slavs, Caucasians and Their Neighbors;
    Greece; Rome.
  - **Vol. 2:** Western Civilization in the Christian Era; South Asia, Iran, and Buddhism;
    Southeast Asia; East Asia and Inner Asia; The Americas and the South Pacific.
- **Stance:** Elite academic, **deliberately non-exhaustive** — instead of cataloging every deity,
  it studies "major structures or problems" through essayistic articles: a structuralist/
  problem-oriented, comparative approach drawing on history of religions, anthropology, archaeology,
  linguistics.

### 10. Wörterbuch der Mythologie (H. W. Haussig) — German, multi-volume by region

- **Editors:** Hans Wilhelm Haussig and Egidius Schmalzriedt.
- **Publisher / dates:** Ernst Klett Verlag / Klett-Cotta, Stuttgart, from 1965 (volumes into the
  1990s).
- **Organizing principle:** **Regional volumes**, each subdivided into cultural sections,
  internally an alphabetical dictionary of names/motifs. Planned in three divisions; **only Division
  I, "Die alten Kulturvölker," was realized.** The Greek-Roman volume (Band 3) was announced but
  **never appeared** — so the project is **incomplete.**
- **Published volumes (Division I):** Bd. 1 *Vorderer Orient* (Mesopotamia, Anatolia, Syria/Ugarit,
  Egypt, Arabia, Ethiopia), 1965; Bd. 2 *Altes Europa* (Germanic, Celtic, Slavic, Hungarian,
  Finnish, Baltic, Albanian, Basque, Berber, Iberian), 1973; Bd. 3 Greek-Roman — never published;
  Bd. 4 *kaukasische und iranische Völker*, 1986; Bd. 5 *indischer Subkontinent*, 1984; Bd. 6
  *Ostasien* (Shinto, Ainu, Korean, Chinese Buddhist, Taoist), 1994; Bd. 7/I–II *Zentralasien und
  Nordeurasien* (Manchu-Tungus, Turkic, Uralic/Siberian, Sami, Bon, Tibetan, Mongolian), 1998–1999.
- **Stance:** Rigorous German **historical-philological** scholarship. Deep per-region but skewed to
  the literate Old World; the Americas, Sub-Saharan Africa (beyond Berber), and Oceania are largely
  absent because the project stalled in Division I.

### 11. Yuri Berezkin — Electronic Analytic Catalogue of Folklore and Mythology Motifs

- **Creator:** Yuri E. Berezkin (anthropologist/folklorist, Kunstkamera / St. Petersburg).
- **Scale:** **~50,000 abstracts of oral texts worldwide**; parsed from ~6,239 books/articles by
  ~4,041 authors in 32 languages; **~2,564 motifs** coded and linked to **~958 ethnolinguistic
  groups**; ~2,000 cosmological/etiological/trickster motifs checked across ~1,000 traditions.
- **Method:** A **motif-and-areal-distribution** database, not a narrative encyclopedia. A "motif"
  = an episode or image recurring in a community's narratives; motifs are mapped geographically to
  reconstruct deep-time cultural history and migrations (famously the peopling of the New World from
  folklore-motif distributions). Began with the Americas, then extended to the Old World.
- **Stance:** A highly regarded quantitative/cartographic approach — the largest single-scholar
  comparative motif dataset. Its carving of the world is **by ethnolinguistic group and geographic
  area** rather than literate/oral prestige: a deliberate counter to the literate-centric
  encyclopedias.

### 12. Aarne–Thompson–Uther (ATU) Index & Thompson's Motif-Index

- **ATU Index — *The Types of International Folktales*:** Antti Aarne (1910) → expanded by Stith
  Thompson (1928, rev. 1961) → thoroughly revised by **Hans-Jörg Uther (2004), 3 volumes** (FFC);
  updated 2024 edition. Classifies tale *types* into ~seven broad categories (Animal Tales; Tales of
  Magic; Religious Tales; Realistic/Novelle; Tales of the Stupid Ogre; Anecdotes/Jokes; Formula
  Tales).
- **Motif-Index of Folk-Literature (Stith Thompson):** **6 volumes** (1932–1936; rev. 1955–1958), a
  granular classification of narrative *motifs* (A cosmology/creation, B animals, … through Z),
  cross-indexed with the tale-type index.
- **Organizing principle:** **Numeric/alphabetic classification of narrative content** (types and
  motifs), transcending region — a comparative *tool*, not a mythography, and the foundational
  infrastructure for comparative folklore (and for Berezkin's and other digital catalogues).
  Historically Euro-centric in coverage, which Uther's revision worked to broaden.

### 13. ARAS — Archive for Research in Archetypal Symbolism

- **What it is:** An encyclopedic image archive of ~**18,000 photographic images** of art, ritual
  objects, and artifacts from all cultures and epochs (Paleolithic to modern), each with scholarly
  and archetypal commentary, cultural history, bibliography, and glossary.
- **Organizing principle:** Cross-indexed by symbol/image; online as a subscription database.
- **Stance:** **Jungian / archetypal** — associated with the C. G. Jung Institutes; interprets
  images for their "transpersonal and symbolic" meaning across cultures. Valuable as a visual/
  symbolic reference, but theoretically committed to a Jungian universalism that mainstream
  religious-studies scholarship treats cautiously.

### 14. Theoi.com (Theoi Project)

- **Creator:** Aaron J. Atsma (Auckland), with University of Auckland and Leiden University library
  support; established 2000.
- **Scale / scope:** Largest free web reference for **Greek mythology only**: **1,500+ pages,
  1,200+ images**; a page per deity/creature with encyclopedia summary, extensive **primary-source
  quotations**, and ancient art. Also hosts a library of classical texts.
- **Stance:** Source-driven catalog of the Greek pantheon; explicitly **presents primary sources,
  not interpretation.** Reliable and heavily used, but single-tradition and non-analytical. Its very
  existence — an entire large site for Greece alone — is the extreme case of the documentary bias.

### 15. Database of Religious History (DRH)

- **What it is:** UBC-based (director Edward Slingerland), launched 2012 — "the world's first
  comprehensive, online quantitative and qualitative encyclopedia of religious and social history."
- **Scale:** **200+ religious groups/traditions**, contributions from ~150–500+ scholars/experts.
- **Method:** A **standardized expert "poll"** — questions about religious groups across time/space
  (mostly pre-modern), coded Yes/No with qualitative comment boxes and references, enabling
  comparative analysis and visualization.
- **Stance:** Peer-expert-sourced, cultural-evolution/quantitative comparative religion. Carves the
  world by **religious group/entity** (not region or pantheon), designed for statistical analysis
  rather than narrative reading.

## Part II — Cross-cutting synthesis

### A. How the major works divide world mythology

| Work | Organizing unit | Top-level divisions | Granularity pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| **New Larousse** (Guirand) | Region/culture essays | ~17–18: Prehistoric, Egyptian, Assyro-Babylonian, Phoenician, Greek, Roman, Celtic, Teutonic, Slavonic, Finno-Ugric, Persia, Indian, Chinese, Japanese, "Two Americas," Oceanic, "Black Africa" | Fine for literate Old World; whole New World / Oceania / Africa each one chapter |
| **Mythology of All Races** | One+ region per volume | 12 topical vols: Greek-Roman; Eddic; Celtic+Slavic; Finno-Ugric+Siberian; Semitic; Indian+Iranian; Armenian+African; Chinese+Japanese; Oceanic; N. American; Latin American; Egyptian+Indo-Chinese | Same skew: N. & Latin America = 2 vols total; Africa shares a vol; Oceania one vol |
| **Мифы народов мира** (Tokarev) | A–Z + synthetic per-people articles | Alphabetical, but with large survey entries for every people/region incl. Siberian, Caucasian, Paleo-Asiatic, Australian, African peoples | Most *even* coverage; strong on Eurasian/Siberian/oral traditions |
| **Bonnefoy / *Mythologies*** (Chicago) | Problem-essays, regrouped by region | Vol 1: Africa; Ancient Near East; Celts-Norse-Slavs-Caucasians; Greece; Rome. Vol 2: Christian-era West; South Asia/Iran/Buddhism; SE Asia; East & Inner Asia; Americas & South Pacific | Finer than Larousse in Asia; still pools "Americas & South Pacific" |
| **Haussig, Wörterbuch der Mythologie** | Regional volumes (Div. I only) | Ancient Near East; Old Europe; (Greek-Roman never issued); Caucasian & Iranian; Indian subcontinent; East Asia; Central Asia & N. Eurasia | Old-World-only; **no** Americas / Sub-Saharan Africa / Pacific (project stalled) |
| **Cotterell, *Dict. of World Mythology*** (OUP) | Region-grouped A–Z | 7: West Asia; South & Central Asia; East Asia; Europe; America; Africa; Oceania | Coarse continental buckets |
| **Encyclopedia of Religion** (Eliade/Jones) | A–Z, cross-cultural | No regional top level — alphabetical entries on traditions, deities, concepts, methods worldwide | Even by intent; comparative-religion frame |
| **Berezkin catalogue** | Ethnolinguistic group × motif | ~958 groups grouped by geographic/areal zones worldwide | Deliberately fine and even for *oral* traditions |
| **DRH** | Religious group/entity | 200+ groups, no regional hierarchy | Analysis-driven, group-level |

**The recurring pattern:** the print encyclopedias (Larousse, Mythology of All Races, Cotterell,
Haussig, and to a lesser degree Bonnefoy) **split the literate Old World finely** — Greek, Roman,
Egyptian, Mesopotamian/Semitic, Persian/Iranian, Indian, Chinese, Japanese each get their own
section — but **pool oral / non-literate regions coarsely.** "The Two Americas / the Americas,"
"Oceania / the South Pacific," and "(Black/Sub-Saharan) Africa" repeatedly appear as single
undivided units, despite each containing hundreds of distinct traditions.

### B. What earns a tradition its own section vs. pooling?

The decisive factor is **the volume of documented, written textual material** — not the number of
ethnic groups or the intrinsic richness of the mythology.

- Traditions with **large surviving written corpora** (Homer and Hesiod for Greece, the Egyptian
  and Mesopotamian texts, the Vedas and Sanskrit epics, the Chinese and Japanese classics, the Norse
  Eddas) are split finely — each gets a chapter, volume, or region because there is a canonical
  literature to expound. Theoi.com (an entire large site for Greece alone) is the extreme case.
- Traditions transmitted **orally** and documented mainly through ethnography — Sub-Saharan Africa,
  the Indigenous Americas, Oceania, Siberia/Central Asia — are pooled, even though they encompass
  far *more* distinct ethnic groups and languages than the literate blocks.
- This is a **documentary/philological bias, not a demographic one**: sections track the density of
  *written sources and prior scholarship*, so literate civilizations with deep textual and academic
  traditions get disproportionate space.
- The notable **counter-examples** correct for this by changing the organizing unit. **Мифы народов
  мира** (built by the Moscow–Tartu semioticians) gives serious, even coverage to Siberian,
  Caucasian, Paleo-Asiatic, Australian, and African peoples; **Berezkin's catalogue** and the
  **ATU/Thompson** apparatus abandon the pantheon/text unit entirely for the *motif* and the
  *ethnolinguistic group*, which lets oral traditions register at the same resolution as literate
  ones. The DRH similarly uses the *religious group* as its atom.

### C. Alphabetical dictionary vs. region essay — tradeoffs

**Alphabetical A–Z** (Encyclopedia of Religion, Funk & Wagnalls, Мифы народов мира, Man Myth &
Magic, Lurker/Routledge, original French Bonnefoy):

- *Strengths:* fast look-up of a specific name/term; forces cross-cultural juxtaposition (Isis next
  to Ishtar); good for reference and comparison; scales to many small traditions without a prior
  taxonomy.
- *Weaknesses:* fragments each tradition into scattered entries; hard to read a coherent narrative
  of one mythology; presupposes the user already knows the name; foregrounds isolated figures over
  systems and structures.

**Region/culture essays** (New Larousse, Mythology of All Races, Haussig, Cotterell's grouped
scheme, the restructured English *Mythologies*):

- *Strengths:* each tradition is presented as a coherent system with context and internal relations;
  better for learning a mythology whole; lets a specialist give a synthetic account; good for
  narrative reading.
- *Weaknesses:* slow to find one figure; the section boundaries **encode and entrench the
  literate/oral bias** (you must decide up front how to carve the world, and coarse buckets result);
  cross-cultural comparison is harder; less flexible as new traditions are added.

**The hybrid that works best:** Мифы народов мира (A–Z *plus* large synthetic per-people articles)
and *Mythologies* (essay-articles regrouped regionally for the English edition) show the two
principles can be combined — quick entry access plus coherent regional synthesis. The purest analytic
answer, though, is the **motif/database** model (Berezkin, ATU, the Motif-Index, DRH): by indexing
*content* rather than assuming a geographic table of contents, it sidesteps the "how to carve the
world" problem altogether and treats oral and literate traditions at equal resolution — at the cost
of narrative readability.

## The literate/oral bias thesis, in one line

Every region-organized reference splits the literate Old World finely and pools the oral world
coarsely, because **sections track surviving written text and prior scholarship, not the number or
richness of traditions.** The only works that achieve even coverage — Мифы народов мира and
Berezkin's catalogue — do so by changing the atom of description: from the pantheon or the canonical
text to the *per-people synthetic article* and the *motif × ethnolinguistic group*.

*A note on confidence:* bibliographic facts (editors, dates, volume and entry counts) are
cross-checked against publisher pages, library catalogs, and Wikipedia. Entry-count figures for older
works (Leach ~4,000–8,000; Larousse ~17–18 sections) vary by edition and are approximate. Haussig's
incompleteness (no Greek-Roman volume, only Division I published) and the Мифы народов мира entry
count (>12,000) are as reported by the German and Russian Wikipedias respectively.
