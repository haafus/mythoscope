What we found

Comparative mythology has always wanted one answer: given a motif's spread across the world, how old is it, and how did it get where it is? Mythoscope put that question to a global motif corpus and let the numbers fall where they fell. The short version is honest rather than triumphant. Most myth travels sideways, by contact. A thin minority is genuinely inherited, and that minority can be dated. A small deep layer really does reach across the hemispheres. And beyond a certain point, the question stops having an answer the distribution can give — a limit we report as carefully as any positive result.

Four findings, one boundary. Here is the whole of it.

The corpus at a glance
2,311 / 2,775 motifs are areal-dominant — spread by neighbourhood and distance, not descent
≈ 1% are broad and clade-clustered: the Eurasian fairy-tale core, datable to ≈ 5,500 BP
320 / 480 both-hemisphere motifs survive every de-confounding control — the deep substrate
≈ 0.16 vs 0.16 descent share of the deepest cosmology and a late fairy tale — indistinguishable

Everything below unpacks these four tiles, and the one place where the numbers run out.

1. Geography wins

When each motif is placed on the tree of languages and asked whether its carriers cluster by descent or scatter by contact, the verdict is lopsided. A continuous three-way split — decomposing every motif into descent, areal, and reinvention shares — puts 2,311 of 2,775 motifs in the areal-dominant class, against 460 descent-dominant and a mere 4 dominated by independent reinvention. Isolation by distance and neighbour diffusion is not one mode among several; it is the mode.

This is deflationary for anyone hoping the corpus is mostly a fossil record. Cosmology, the trickster, and the famous swan-maiden are all widespread — but their footprints are areally scattered, not clade-clustered. Breadth, it turns out, is mostly manufactured by contact, and contact manufactures it fast.

One guardrail is worth naming, because it is where a looser analysis would have gone wrong. A per-tradition version of this decomposition was tried first and rejected: for a broad motif, "has a same-family relative that also carries it" is trivially true — Galton's problem again — so descent and diffusion become unidentifiable and the model over-credits inheritance. Anchoring the split at the motif level, in a chance-corrected phylogenetic signal that does not saturate with breadth, is what makes the 2,311 trustworthy.

2. A datable one percent

Against that areal background stands a well-defined descent minority — on the order of one motif in a hundred that is both broad enough to matter and clustered on the language tree tightly enough to be inheritance rather than borrowing. And it is not a random one percent. It is the Eurasian fairy-tale core — Cinderella, the "seven at a blow" boastful-weakling tale — motifs that track the branching of language families. This is an independent recovery, from a whole areal catalogue rather than a hand-picked tale-type set, of the published finding that märchen follow language phylogeny. The corpus confirms it without being tuned to.

Where the mode resolves, ordinal depth can become calendar age. Joining each tradition to its nearest language and calibrating against published family-expansion dates, 451 of some 3,000 datable motifs receive a calendar age — ranging from about 9,000 years before present for Afro-Asiatic material down to about 1,500 for Quechuan, and piling up, some three hundred of them, at the Indo-European märchen belt around 5,500 BP.

The dating rule is deliberately strict: a motif is dated to a family's spread only if it is both phylogenetically clustered and concentrated in that one family. The areal majority is correctly left undated — the sun-and-moon pair and the swan-maiden have low phylogenetic signal and cross families rather than riding one, so the method assigns them no date. Dating an areally diffused motif by clade depth would be meaningless, and the gated method knows not to try.

A striking check falls out for free. Theme was never fed to the estimator, yet the cosmological share of a motif's content falls from 64% in the deep-areal mode to 24% in the descent mode. The descent minority is disproportionately narrative (fairy tales); the deep-areal material is disproportionately cosmological — and the model recovered that split without ever being told what any motif was about.

3. A deep spine, not a skeleton

The most contested claim in the field is that beneath the areal churn lies a deep, trans-hemispheric substrate — chiefly celestial cosmology, old enough to predate the peopling of the continents. The corpus supports a version of this, but a chastened one, and only after two mandatory controls.

Coverage weighting shrinks apparent breadth by about 31% once densely-catalogued traditions stop inflating every motif's range; a banality proxy flags motifs simple enough to be reinvented. Under both, 504 motifs change mode, mostly sliding from areal-broad to areal-recent. That is the sound of a great deal of apparent antiquity evaporating.

And yet the spine holds. Of the 480 motifs that touch both hemispheres, 320 survive both controls. The deep trans-hemispheric class is real — its reality established precisely by what remains after the controls take their share.

It is simply small — a spine, not a skeleton — and its reality is established precisely by the fact that it is what remains after the controls have taken their share.

That the survivors are not a coding artefact of one catalogue is confirmed through the crosswalk: just under half of all motifs — 48% — are corroborated by an independent index, and the corroboration is theme-blind (cosmological motifs 49%, tale-types 49%), so the deep-substrate finding is not an artefact of one cataloguer's idiosyncrasies. It runs higher still for the broad motifs the claim rests on (54%) than for the narrow tail of fine variants (20%). And 48% is a lower bound: the crosswalk is an automated match with known false negatives, so the true corroborated share is higher.

A prediction that could have failed did not. If depth scores mean anything, traditions rich in deep motifs should sit in the earliest-peopled regions. They do: deep-share tracks first-peopling age with a partial correlation of +0.48 — Sub-Saharan Africa highest (63% deep-share, settled ~65,000 years ago), the Americas lowest (48–49%, settled ~14,000 years ago). Tellingly, coverage was masking this signal, not faking it: a thickly-catalogued corpus like Europe records so many rare motifs it looks artificially shallow, so controlling for coverage strengthens the correlation. De-confounding is not a machine for shrinking effects; it is a machine for finding their true size.

4. The irreducible limit

Now the negative result — which the programme regards as one of its central contributions rather than a disappointment.

The sun and moon imagined as kin (catalogued A3), the prime candidate for the deepest cosmological substrate, and the swan-maiden (K25), a candidate for a much later fairy-tale spread, receive near-identical mixtures — a descent component of about 0.16 apiece. The model cannot tell them apart. One may be a hundred-thousand-year-old inheritance and the other a few-thousand-year-old diffusion, and from their distributions alone they look the same.

This is not a failure of a particular method; it is a property of the evidence. A deep shared substrate and a widely diffused innovation produce the same kind of footprint — broad, disjunct, areally scattered — and no score computed from that footprint can separate them.

Could content supply the missing signal? One might hope so. It cannot. Nearest-by-meaning motifs do share a theme group 58% of the time against a 20% chance baseline — a factor of 2.9, confirming the embeddings are valid and that content is nearly the theme axis itself. But meaning barely predicts age: content correlates only about r ≈ 0.28 with breadth and 0.18 with prevalence.

What a motif is about is not how old it is.

Separating A3 from K25 requires stepping outside the distribution entirely — to genetics, historical corridors, node-level dated phylogenies. That boundary is named, not tuned away.

What it adds up to

Three findings cut across everything, and they reconcile a century-old standoff.

Geography is primary; descent is a minority mode; deep inheritance is real but small. This picture is consistent with, and quantifies, both the phylogenetic programme that finds descent in Indo-European tales and the Pleistocene-substrate hypotheses that posit a deep global layer — while showing that the two operate on largely disjoint slices of the corpus.

Neither camp was wrong; each described a different one percent.

Depth is a property of a distribution, not of meaning, and it is only partly recoverable. No linear score, no embedding, no single facet dates a motif. What keeps this from becoming an excuse for over-fitting is the discipline of the falsifiable gate. When connectivity was modelled, a coarse landscape-friction upgrade failed a test set for it in advance — great-circle distance beat resistance distance out of sample across all three sea regimes — and only dated historical empires added real cross-area signal, and only where they physically reached (traditions in different macro-areas that once shared an empire share ×2.6 more motifs, against a negligible global effect). Reporting the clean negatives is the main thing standing between a programme like this and a compelling story fitted to noise.

Depth is a property of a distribution, not of meaning.

A catalogue's theme axis encodes a choice, and there are two good ones. The classical scheme orders motifs by what a myth explains; a bottom-up axis re-derived from meaning orders them by how a tale is built. They are orthogonal — the narrative axis is the better descriptor of a tradition (and nearly subsumes the classical one, whose unique contribution collapses to 0.003 when both are in the model), while the etiological axis remains the better reader of geography. The practical answer is to keep both.

The honest caveats

  • Induction from text is unfinished. These findings describe the distribution of motifs in an inherited catalogue. Growing the corpus by inducing motifs from raw text — checked against a gold standard, beating the field's stubborn simple baselines, and consulted with culture-bearers where meaning cannot be read off the page — is the principal open goal, not a solved problem. The unit of analysis is, for now, inherited rather than independently validated at scale.
  • The residual is two-thirds of the structure. A tradition's describable facets recover only about a third of why traditions share the motifs they do. The connectivity channels closed a sliver of the rest; the deep-versus-diffuse ambiguity cannot be closed from distribution at all. That convergence residual is not the programme's failure — it is its map of future work, naming exactly the external joins (fine genetics, trade routes, node-level dated trees) that would light it up.
  • Dates are family-resolution. The 451 calendar ages are point estimates at family resolution, not node-consistent reconstructions with proper uncertainty. A dated tree and full Bayesian phylogeography would sharpen them.
