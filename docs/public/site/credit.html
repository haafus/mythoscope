Credit & authorship

Academic work is credited work, and credit that is left to discretion is credit that goes missing. This page sets out, in advance and in writing, how contribution to Mythoscope converts into recognition — from a name in a contributor list, through a citable dataset, to co-authorship — and the roles and criteria by which those lines are drawn. It applies to everyone: corpus contributors, annotators, methods and code contributors, and community partners.

The policy is mirrored in the repository (CONTRIBUTORS.md records everyone credited; CITATION.cff records how to cite the software and data), so the public statement and the machine-readable record agree.

The attribution ladder

Credit scales with the nature and weight of a contribution. There are three rungs, and a contribution can move up as it grows:

  1. Acknowledgement. Every contribution is recorded. Anyone who adds a text, an annotation, a fix, a dataset, or a review is listed as a contributor (in CONTRIBUTORS.md and, where relevant, in the acknowledgements of the work it fed). This is the floor, not a courtesy — nothing goes uncredited.

  2. Data / tool citation. When a contribution is a distinct, reusable object — a curated corpus for a tradition, a substantial annotation layer, a dataset, a tool — it is released as its own citable artifact with its own DOI, so the contributor is cited directly whenever their object is used, independently of any paper. This is how someone who builds a resource gets the standing academic reward for building a resource: a citation, not a footnote. (DOIs are minted on the first archived release; see Publications.)

  3. Co-authorship. When a contribution is substantial in the sense set out below — curating a tradition's corpus end-to-end, a methods contribution that shapes a result, sustained interpretive work that turns a distributional pattern into a finding — the contributor is a co-author of the work it belongs to, on the criteria below.

The rungs are cumulative and honest: the aim is that the credit a person receives matches the work they did, and that the match is legible to a hiring or tenure committee.

Roles: the CRediT taxonomy

To keep credit transparent and granular — rather than collapsing everyone into a single undifferentiated author list — Mythoscope uses the CRediT (Contributor Roles Taxonomy) standard. Each contributor's roles are recorded, so it is clear what each person did. The fourteen roles are:

  • Conceptualization — ideas, framing, research goals.
  • Data curation — corpus building, cleaning, annotation, provenance, maintenance.
  • Formal analysis — statistical, computational, or mathematical analysis.
  • Funding acquisition — securing financial support.
  • Investigation — conducting the research; data collection.
  • Methodology — designing the methods and models.
  • Project administration — coordination and management.
  • Resources — providing texts, tools, computing, or other materials.
  • Software — programming, implementation, testing, maintenance.
  • Supervision — oversight and mentorship.
  • Validation — verifying results and reproducibility.
  • Visualization — figures, maps, interactive views.
  • Writing – original draft — drafting the work.
  • Writing – review & editing — critical revision.

A single contribution often carries more than one role, and roles are named in the outputs so that "author" is never a black box.

Criteria for co-authorship

Co-authorship follows adapted ICMJE-style thresholds. A co-author is someone who meets substantially all of the following:

  1. Substantial contribution to conception or design, or to the acquisition, analysis, or interpretation of the data — corresponding to rung 3 of the ladder above.
  2. Intellectual involvement in drafting the work or revising it critically for important content.
  3. Approval of the version to be published.
  4. Accountability for the work — willing to stand behind the part they contributed and to help ensure questions about its accuracy or integrity are addressed.

Contributions that do not meet all four — providing a text, a one-off fix, or general support — are recognised at rungs 1–2 (acknowledgement and/or data citation), which is the appropriate and full credit for that kind of work, not a lesser version of authorship. Order of authorship and specific roles are agreed among contributors, using CRediT to make the division explicit rather than implicit.

CARE principles for Indigenous data

Some of the material Mythoscope works with belongs to living communities, and open-data defaults are not sufficient for it. Alongside the FAIR data principles (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable), the project follows the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance:

  • Collective benefit — data ecosystems are arranged so that Indigenous communities benefit from data drawn from or about them, not only outside researchers.
  • Authority to control — communities hold the authority to govern how their cultural material, knowledge, and representations are used; consent is prior and informed.
  • Responsibility — those working with the data are accountable for how it is used and for supporting the community's self-determination.
  • Ethics — the rights and wellbeing of the community are the primary concern at every stage.

In practice this means: community control over how a tradition is represented, the right to review interpretations before they are published, proper attribution to the community and named knowledge-keepers, benefit-sharing, co-governance of decisions about the material, and the right to have material removed. Community partners are credited on the same ladder as any other contributor — and, where they wish, as co-authors — with these protocols governing throughout. The partnership route is described in Contribute.

Questions

If you have contributed and are unsure where your work sits on the ladder, or want to discuss authorship on a specific output, write to hello@mythoscope.io. The policy is meant to be applied in good faith and in the open; where a case is genuinely borderline, we would rather over-credit than under-credit.
