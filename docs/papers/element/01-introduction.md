## 1.1 Why a motif recurs

A folklore motif is a small, portable unit of narrative — the swan-maiden who sheds her feather
cloak and is trapped by the man who hides it; the theft of fire from its first jealous owner; the sun
and the moon imagined as siblings, or as a quarrelling married pair. Such units recur across the
world's traditions with a stubbornness that has fascinated and unsettled comparative scholarship for
two centuries. The same motif turns up among peoples who, as far as any record shows, never met. The
question this book takes as its starting point is the oldest question the comparative study of myth
can ask: **why?**

There are only three general answers, and every particular case is some mixture of them. A motif may
be **inherited** — carried down within a lineage of tradition, passed from one generation to the next
along with the language and the rest of the culture, so that its distribution today traces the
branching history of a family of peoples. This is descent, and where it holds, a motif behaves like a
heritable character on a family tree: fairy tales within the Indo-European world are the textbook
case (Tehrani 2013; da Silva & Tehrani 2016). A motif may instead **diffuse** — travel sideways
between neighbouring peoples irrespective of language or ancestry, carried by trade, conquest,
intermarriage, and the ordinary porousness of adjacent communities, so that its distribution tracks
geography rather than genealogy. Or a motif may be **reinvented** — arising more than once,
independently, because it answers to something general about human minds, human environments, or the
narrative form itself: the night sky is available to everyone, and so is death, and so is the puzzle
of where people came from.

Distinguishing these three modes in any given case is not a matter of taste. It is a hard inferential
problem, and it has a name. When two traditions share a trait, the resemblance may reflect common
descent, or borrowing across a boundary, or independent convergence — and the naïve tally that treats
each tradition as an independent data point silently assumes the answer is convergence, when it may
be neither. This is **Galton's problem** (Naroll 1961), first raised in exactly this comparative
setting: Francis Galton objected, at an 1889 reading of a cross-cultural correlation, that the cases
were not independent because neighbours copy one another and relatives inherit from a common source.
The objection is fatal to any comparative claim that does not confront it. A striking pan-continental
distribution can be the fingerprint of a single ancient origin — or the accumulated residue of
countless local borrowings that never had a common source at all. The two look alike on a map. Much
of the analytical work in this book is, at bottom, a sustained effort to hold Galton's problem in
view and to report honestly when the data cannot tell the two apart (Figure 1.1).

The stakes are not merely methodological. Behind the three modes stand three quite different pictures
of the human past. If descent dominates, then myth is a slow-changing heritable system, and its
distribution is a kind of fossil record from which the deep history of peoples might be read. If
diffusion dominates, then myth is a fluid, contact-driven medium, and its map is a map of who spoke
to whom. If reinvention dominates, then the recurrences are telling us about the human mind rather
than human history — about cognitive attractors and narrative universals — and the comparative
enterprise, in its historical form, dissolves. The strong claims at the far ends of this
spectrum — that a common stock of motifs was carried out of Africa with the first modern humans and
survives in the mythologies of every continent (Berezkin 2015; d'Huy 2013), or, conversely, that
apparent universals are independent reinventions of a few cognitively favoured shapes — are claims
about which of the three modes governs the deepest layer of the corpus. They cannot be settled by
accumulating more parallels. They can only be approached by asking, of each motif, what the *shape*
of its distribution across the world is actually consistent with.

That phrase — the shape of a distribution — is the hinge of the whole book. A motif is not just
present or absent in the world; it is present in a particular pattern: concentrated or scattered,
confined to one continent or straddling several, clustered on a language tree or smeared across it
regardless of language. These patterns are the only direct evidence we have of the three modes at
work. The central wager of this book is that the modes leave legible signatures in the geometry of a
distribution — and that where they do not, honesty requires saying so.

## 1.2 Three obstacles

If the shape of a distribution carries the signal, then the obvious research programme is to assemble
the distributions at scale and read the signal off. Three obstacles have stood in the way, and each
has to be dealt with before any claim can be trusted.

**The catalogues do not interoperate.** The comparative study of narrative has produced several great
reference works, but they were built on different principles and do not speak to one another.
Thompson's *Motif-Index of Folk-Literature* (Thompson 1955–58) is an enormous, finely subdivided
taxonomy of narrative elements, organised by subject; the Aarne–Thompson–Uther index (Uther 2004)
catalogues whole *tale types* rather than motifs; and Yuri Berezkin's areal catalogue, uniquely,
records not just motifs but their **distribution over ethnographic traditions**, placing each of some
three and a half thousand motifs on a map of roughly a thousand peoples. These indices carve the
material at different joints, in different languages, with different conventions. To study
distributions across the whole field, they must first be linked: a motif in one must be connected to
its counterpart in another wherever such a counterpart exists. Building and validating that link
structure — a crosswalk — is a precondition for everything else.

**Coverage is deeply uneven.** A catalogue records what someone has recorded. Europe has been
combed for folklore for two centuries; many other traditions have been documented thinly, or once, or
by a single collector with idiosyncratic interests. The result is that raw counts confound history
with effort. A rare motif attested in a densely catalogued tradition and absent from a thinly
catalogued one may reflect a true difference in the myths — or merely the fact that one was written
down more thoroughly than the other. The number of motifs recorded for a tradition ranges, in the
corpus used here, across more than two orders of magnitude. Any analysis that does not correct for
this will discover, again and again, the shape of the collecting enterprise rather than the shape of
the myths. De-confounding sampling is therefore not an optional robustness check bolted on at the
end; it is a discipline that has to run through every step, and a claim that does not survive it is
not reported as a finding.

**The theme axis has never been checked.** The standard way of grouping motifs by subject — cosmogony,
the origin of death, the origin of humans, tricksters, and so on — is a scholar's ordering, inherited
from the structure of the classical indices. It is intuitive, it is useful, and it has never been
tested against the structure the material itself carries. Do motifs actually cluster, in their
meaning and their distribution, along the lines the traditional themes draw? Or do those themes cut
across the natural grain of the corpus, lumping together things that belong apart and splitting things
that belong together? Until the question is asked with the material allowed to answer, any result that
leans on the thematic categories is resting on an untested assumption.

These three obstacles are not independent nuisances to be cleared away before the real work begins.
They are structural features of the object of study, and how one handles them largely determines what
one is permitted to conclude. A great deal of the apparent disagreement in comparative mythology — between
those who see a deep universal substrate and those who see local convergence — turns out, on
inspection, to be disagreement about sampling, about interoperability, and about whether the
categories being compared are natural or imposed. This book treats all three as first-class problems.

## 1.3 The thesis

The argument of this book can be put in a single paragraph, and every chapter that follows is in the
service of it. **On a large, cross-indexed, sampling-corrected corpus of the world's folklore motifs,
the geography of a myth is written primarily in *where* it is attested, not in *what* it says.** Areal
diffusion is the dominant mode: most motifs are shaped by neighbourhood and distance, not by descent.
A well-defined but small **descent minority** — on the order of one motif in a hundred — tracks the
branching of language families, is concentrated in the Eurasian fairy-tale tradition, and can be
dated. Beneath both lies a faint **trans-hemispheric substrate**, chiefly celestial cosmology, that
is real — it survives every de-confounding control we can impose and it aligns with the order in which
the continents were peopled — but that is *small*, and whose deep antiquity can never be fully
separated, from distribution alone, from mere wide diffusion. Depth, in short, is a property of a
motif's distribution, not of its meaning, and it is only partly recoverable. And when the theme axis
is re-derived from the material itself, it splits in two: a tradition is best *described* by the
narrative *form* of its corpus — how its tales are built — while the classical *etiological* themes —
what its myths explain — remain the better instrument for reading *geography*. Two orthogonal axes of
theme, one small deep spine, a dominant areal signal, and a large, honestly unexplained residual of
cross-continental convergence: that is the shape of myth in this corpus.

## 1.4 What this Element does, and how it proceeds

This Element is a short book with a single argument, assembled from a larger research programme. It
does not attempt to survey world mythology, nor to interpret any particular tradition, nor to settle
the grand narrative of human dispersal. It does something narrower and, we hope, more durable: it
takes the three catalogues as given, links them into one corpus, joins them to external ethnographic,
linguistic, and historical data, and then asks — with the sampling problem and Galton's problem held
in view throughout — what the *distributions* will and will not support. Where the data speaks
clearly, we report it; where it cannot distinguish two histories, we say so and mark the boundary.
The clean negatives are as much a part of the contribution as the positive findings, because a field
that reports only its successes cannot be trusted with a question as seductive as the origin of myth.

A word on what the Element is *not* yet. Behind the analyses stands a larger machine whose ultimate
purpose is to **induce motifs directly from raw text** rather than take them from a curated index.
That machine is built, but the task is hard — it is, in fact, one of the open problems of the field —
and its validated output does not yet exist at scale. This book is therefore honest about its
substrate: every substantive result rests on the already-curated indices, chiefly Berezkin's coded
distributions, and the induction of motifs from text is described as built infrastructure and staged
as future work (Chapters 4 and 10), not presented as a delivered result. The reader is never asked to
take an unbuilt thing on faith.

The remaining nine chapters proceed as a single line of argument.

**Chapter 2** places the book in its field, computational folkloristics, giving the reader the two
eras of that field in one sitting and its hard-won lesson: that automatic reading of motifs from text
remains unsolved, and that machine representations of meaning serve best as a search-and-retrieval
layer over expert judgement, not as a replacement for it.

**Chapter 3** states the method and the stance. It argues that comparative mythology is a
natural-history science and should walk the arc every observational discipline walked — collect,
describe, classify, explain — as a dependency order rather than a schedule; it lays out the simple
data model in which a motif's time-depth is the one inferred quantity; and it makes explicit the
assumptions and the falsifiable gates under which everything that follows is produced.

**Chapter 4** describes the infrastructure: how the three catalogues are assembled into one corpus,
how they are linked, how every motif is given a machine representation of its meaning and that
representation is validated, and how the external data is joined. This is the chapter that lets a
reader reuse what we built.

**Chapter 5** establishes that the corpus carries real, region-coherent structure that is not an
artefact of uneven collecting, and works out what a *tradition* is — which of its describable
facets carry signal and which are redundant, and how much of the whole they leave unexplained.

**Chapter 6** is the core. It reads a motif's depth two ways — from the shape of its distribution and
from its place on the language tree — and reaches the book's central results: that areal diffusion
dominates, that a small descent minority tracks language and can be dated, that a faint deep substrate
is real but small, and that the difference between a deep shared inheritance and a widely diffused
innovation is, past a certain point, irreducible from distribution alone.

**Chapter 7** confronts the large residual the descriptors leave behind, and tests two ways to close
it — physical landscape connectivity and historical empire — reporting that the first fails a test set
in advance and the second only partly succeeds.

**Chapter 8** returns to the theme axis and re-derives it from the material, showing that there are
two good axes, not one, and that each is better at a different job.

**Chapter 9** slows down to trace three individual motifs — the swan-maiden, the sun-and-moon
siblings, and the fished-up earth — through the whole apparatus, so that the machinery of the earlier
chapters can be seen working on cases a reader can hold in mind.

**Chapter 10** draws the threads together and ends on the frontier: the residual as a map of what a
finer genetic, linguistic, and historical calibration must next explain, and the open milestones —
chief among them the induction of motifs from raw text — that would carry the programme forward.

The book can be read straight through as one argument, or entered at any result chapter by a reader
who trusts the infrastructure; Chapters 5 through 9 each stand on the foundation laid in 3 and 4. What
holds them together is the single claim of §1.3 — that the history of a myth is legible, when it is
legible at all, in the shape of where it is found.
